## What a meeting looks like

- **Subject.** A short title, the kind someone types into a calendar invite.
- **Date.** A weekday in the first half of 2026, between 2026-01-05 and
  2026-06-30. Spread the batch across the range rather than clustering it.
- **Attendees.** Vary the size sharply. Some meetings are two people working
  something out; some are four or five; at least one across a full corpus is
  most of the company. Draw the attendee list from who would plausibly care:
  a firmware bug is not a meeting the general counsel sits in on.
- **Turns.** What was said, in order, each turn attributed to one attendee.
  Not everyone present has to speak.
- **Decisions.** What the meeting settled, if anything. A meeting that settles
  nothing is realistic and is allowed.
- **Action items.** What people agreed to do, each owned by one attendee.

Every meeting stands alone. It is a snapshot with no knowledge of any other
meeting: never refer to another meeting, never say "as we discussed last
week", and never set up a follow-up that another transcript is expected to
pay off.

## Subject matter

Draw on the whole business, not just engineering standups. Firmware and

backlog, a compliance certification. Meetings can be scheduled or ad hoc, and
they can be tense as easily as cordial.

## Imperfections to plant

A real corpus is not clean, and this one is used to test what happens when it
is not. Plant these deliberately, without ever drawing attention to them:

1. **Contradictory facts.** Two meetings in this batch state something
   incompatible about the same thing — a unit cost, a ship date, a test yield,
   a headcount number. Neither meeting acknowledges the other, because neither
   knows about it.
2. **An unanswered question.** Somewhere, someone asks a direct question that
   is never answered. The conversation moves on. It does not become an action
   item.
3. **A reversed decision.** A decision made in an earlier-dated meeting is
   reached the opposite way in a later-dated one. The later meeting does not
   mention the earlier one — it simply decides differently, as though for the
   first time.
4. **Uneven quality.** Transcript quality varies across the batch, the way it
   does when different rooms and different microphones are involved. Some
   meetings read crisply, with clear attribution and complete sentences.
   Others ramble: people talk past each other, sentences trail off, a turn
   lands mid-thought, a stretch is hard to follow. Do not mark or explain the
   difference.

Write the transcripts, nothing else. No commentary, no notes about what you
planted, no meta-text of any kind.
